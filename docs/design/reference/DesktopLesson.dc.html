Why leap years exist

Earth takes about 365.2422 days to circle the Sun, not a neat 365. A calendar of exactly 365 days slips behind the seasons by almost six hours a year. Left alone, that adds up to a full day every four years.

Julius Caesar’s fix in 46 BC was simple: add one extra day every fourth year. That assumes a year of 365.25 days, about eleven minutes too long. By the 1500s the error had grown to ten days.

The Gregorian calendar of 1582 trimmed the excess. Century years skip the leap day unless they divide evenly by 400. That is why 1900 was not a leap year but 2000 was.

Ask the tutor

You asked: why 400 and not 500?

Over 400 years the rule leaves 97 leap days, so the average year is 365.2425 days. That is only about 26 seconds off the real one.
